OnboardingMonetizationExperimentation

Putting spam-protection choice into onboarding

The setting users skipped, turned into an activation and premium step

GlobalLive worldwide

Blocking spam is Truecaller's core value, but new users used to sail past the setting that controls it. I built the onboarding step where users pick their protection level, from Basic to Max, our paid tier. It started as a single-market test and is now live worldwide.

  • Ran discovery and design iterations with our designer, then A/B tested variants across South Africa, India, Egypt, Malaysia, Nigeria, the EU, and LATAM before each rollout step.
  • Dug into the funnel when results looked off: 93.9% of users left the protection-level screen without clicking Continue. That analysis reshaped the design, and the V2 variant cut deactivation versus control.
  • Made it a premium surface with the Premium team: monthly and yearly pricing tests inside onboarding, deeplinks so a day-1 push brings users back to the setting, and upgrade entry points from the call screen.
  • Kept iterating on words, not just UI: a fraud-warning copy variant trended positive within 34,000 exposures in India, one of the cheapest wins of the year.

Result

Rolled out worldwide. A screen users skipped became both an activation step and a premium entry point, with a push-notification loop that brings users back to it.

ActivationFunnelIndia

Getting more users to grant Caller ID permission

The moment Truecaller actually starts working

+15%Grant Rate (India)

Caller ID permission is the moment Truecaller starts working. Most funnel work chases decimals; this one didn't.

  • Analyzed where new users in India dropped the permission ask, then designed an experiment that asks a second time within onboarding, with a bug fix to cap it there.
  • Ran it through our server-side experimentation platform, starting at 10% exposure and scaling on evidence.

Result

15% improvement in Caller ID grant rate among exposed users in India, our largest market.

ComplianceTrustLegal

Shipping compliance as product, not paperwork

Turning regulator language into testable onboarding

3Jurisdictions, 0 incidents

Between 2025 and 2026 the regulatory ground shifted under onboarding: US state age-verification laws, EU DMA consent, India's DPDPA, and consent logging everywhere. I own that surface.

  • Age verification for Texas and Louisiana: ran requirements with Legal, got Android design approved, wrote backend and iOS tickets, and made the hard call to block affected sign-ups for a few days rather than ship non-compliant.
  • Terms & conditions consent logging: rolled out globally on Android with a 10% holdout to watch registration rate before committing to 100%, then kicked off the iOS build.
  • DMA consent flow and India DPDPA: turned regulator language into testable onboarding designs, including an A/B test to measure the registration cost of each consent variant.

Result

Three jurisdictions covered without a compliance incident, and a repeatable pattern: every legal requirement ships with a holdout so we know exactly what it costs the funnel.

GrowthSecurityLifecycle

Moving millions of users off tampered app versions

Nobody owned the problem, so I picked it up

MillionsUsers migrated

A large user segment was running modified APKs: no updates, no security patches, and revenue leakage. Nobody owned the problem, so I picked it up.

  • Sized the segment, wrote the proposal, and set up tracking (new data field, campaign channel, measurement queries) before touching a single user.
  • Ran a 75,000-user push pilot in 3 languages with a control group, read the honest result (weak reinstall evidence), and adjusted: in-app nudges, localized messaging, and an onboarding intervention so migrated users can't loop back onto the tampered build.
  • Scaled the campaign to the full multi-million-user segment while coordinating Identity, CRM, and data teams.

Result

A one-off cleanup became a standing migration program with its own channel, phased plan, and measurable campaign impact.

RetentionExperimentationGlobal

First-week retention, one experiment at a time

Not one hero launch. A portfolio of 20+ experiments a year.

+24%Verification completion (India)

Retention work rarely has a single hero launch. Mine is a portfolio: 20+ experiments a year across India, Nigeria, Kenya, Colombia, Malaysia, Egypt and more.

  • Moved the backup prompt from onboarding to day 3: D7 retention improved in pilot markets, so we rolled it out across the rest of the world and then tested India separately.
  • Notified users whose verification got throttled so they could finish signing up: 24% more users (14 percentage points) completed verification in India.
  • Ran the app upgrade program for users stuck on old versions: soft nudges, then a hard upgrade wall reaching 2.7 million users with a 41% upgrade rate, paused and reworked when user complaints surfaced a real issue.
  • Killed plenty: a caller ID tutorial that lowered retention, a premium interstitial that didn't convert, a copy test with no lift. Each one documented on Confluence so the team never re-runs a dead idea.

Result

A compounding set of small wins on D7 retention and registration rate, and an experiment log the whole team builds on.

RetentionCRMLifecycle

The first week: lifecycle push notifications

From ad hoc campaigns to an automated system

+1.3%D7 Retention (Nigeria)

New users used to hear nothing from us after install. I built the first-week communication program with our CRM team, then automated it.

  • Designed and tested a 7-day push series: English versus local languages (Hinglish, Arabic and more), always against a holdout group so retention claims held up.
  • Automated the series for all new users in Malaysia and Kenya, then scaled testing across our top 5 markets, replacing one-off manual campaigns.
  • Extended the playbook: a day-1 push about blocking in LATAM, a day-5 referral push, win-back pushes to lapsed users, and large-scale re-engagement coordinated with CRM and Legal.

Result

Overall D7 retention in Nigeria rose 1.3% during testing, and first-week communication went from ad hoc campaigns to an automated system with holdout measurement built in.
